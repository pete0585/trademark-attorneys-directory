# Trademark Attorney: Building a Shortlist That Fits Your Needs

Choosing a trademark attorney is a matching problem before it is a comparison problem. Most people start by searching for names, then try to rank them. A better sequence is to define what you actually need, then build a shortlist of providers who plausibly fit, then compare those few on the same criteria. This guide walks through that sequence. It is general information, not legal advice, and it does not recommend any specific provider. Where your situation involves real risk — a dispute, a deadline, a confusingly similar mark already in use — consult a qualified professional.

## Why a shortlist beats a long list

A long list of names invites comparison on the wrong axis. If you have twenty candidates, you will default to whatever is easiest to compare: a website's polish, a directory ranking, a phone number that appears at the top of results. Those signals are weakly related to whether a particular attorney fits your matter.

A shortlist of three to five candidates forces a different question: which of these fits the work I actually have? That is a question you can answer with a checklist. It is also a question you can revisit as your needs become clearer after an initial conversation.

There is a practical reason too. Comparing five providers on ten criteria is fifty judgments. Comparing twenty on the same criteria is two hundred, and most of them will be guesses. Narrow first, then go deep.

## Step 1: Define the work before you define the provider

Before searching, write down what you think you need. Be specific about the shape of the work, not the label of the service. Consider:

- **What you are protecting.** A word mark, a logo, a slogan, a product name, a packaging design, or some combination.
- **Where you are protecting it.** A single country, a region, or multiple jurisdictions. Cross-border work changes the questions you should ask.
- **What stage you are at.** Pre-filing clearance, an application already filed, a response to an office action, an opposition, a renewal, or a portfolio review.
- **How much you want to be involved.** Some people want to be walked through each step. Others want a clear plan and periodic updates.
- **What your internal capacity is.** If you have in-house counsel or a paralegal, the attorney's role may be narrower than if you are handling everything yourself.

This step is not about diagnosing your legal situation. It is about describing your matter in plain language so that the conversations you have later are grounded in something concrete.

## Step 2: Generate candidates from more than one channel

A shortlist built from a single source tends to inherit that source's biases. If you only use one directory, you see the providers who invest in that directory. If you only ask one colleague, you see the providers that colleague happened to use.

Consider drawing candidates from several channels:

- **Professional directories and bar association resources** that list attorneys by practice area.
- **Referrals from people in similar businesses** — not necessarily similar legal matters, but similar scale and industry.
- **Referrals from adjacent professionals** such as accountants, business formation services, or general counsel who work with trademark matters indirectly.
- **Published writing and speaking** by attorneys on trademark topics relevant to your industry.

At this stage, you are collecting names, not ranking them. Resist the urge to eliminate candidates because their website is unimpressive or because they are not the first result you saw.

## Step 3: Apply a fit filter before a quality filter

Fit questions are faster to answer than quality questions, and they eliminate more candidates. Ask:

- **Does this provider work on matters like mine?** Not "do they do trademarks" but "do they do the kind of trademark work I described in Step 1."
- **Do they work with clients at my scale?** A firm built for large portfolios may not be set up for a single application, and a solo practitioner may not be the right fit for a multi-jurisdiction filing program.
- **Do they cover the jurisdictions I need?** If you need coverage outside their primary jurisdiction, ask how they handle it.
- **Is their communication style compatible with mine?** Some people want detailed written explanations. Others want a short call and a summary email.

A provider can be excellent and still be a poor fit. The fit filter is not a judgment about quality. It is a judgment about match.

## Step 4: Use a checklist to compare the finalists

Once you have three to five candidates, compare them on the same criteria. The checklist below is designed to be used in conversation, not filled out from a website.

### Shortlist comparison checklist

1. **Scope of work.** Can they describe, in plain language, what they would do first and why?
2. **Process clarity.** Do they explain the steps, the likely sequence, and where your input is needed?
3. **Communication cadence.** How often will you hear from them, and through what channel?
4. **Who does the work.** Will the person you speak with handle the matter, or will it be passed to someone else?
5. **Jurisdictional coverage.** If your matter crosses borders, how is that handled?
6. **Cost structure.** Do they explain how fees are structured and what is included, without you having to extract it?
7. **Follow-up expectations.** What happens after the initial filing or the immediate issue is resolved?
8. **Responsiveness.** How quickly did they respond before you were a client?
9. **Questions they ask you.** Do they ask about your business, your plans, and your risk tolerance?
10. **Comfort with uncertainty.** Can they say "I don't know yet" or "that depends" without losing your confidence?

A provider who scores well on all ten is rare. A provider who scores well on the three or four that matter most to you is a strong candidate.

## Hypothetical examples (illustrative only)

The following examples are invented for illustration. They are not case studies, and they do not describe real people, firms, or outcomes.

**Example A: The single-jurisdiction applicant.** A small business owner is filing for a word mark in one country and wants a straightforward application. She builds a shortlist of three attorneys who regularly handle single applications, compares them on process clarity and communication cadence, and chooses the one who explained the sequence most clearly in the initial call. Cost structure matters to her, but it is not the deciding factor.

**Example B: The multi-jurisdiction filer.** A company is expanding into several markets and needs coverage in more than one jurisdiction. Their shortlist includes providers who can coordinate across jurisdictions, and the comparison checklist weights jurisdictional coverage and follow-up expectations more heavily than initial cost. They ask each candidate how they handle local requirements in each market.

**Example C: The office action response.** An applicant has received a response from a trademark office and is unsure what it means. Their shortlist is built around providers who handle responses of that type, and the first conversation is about understanding the issue rather than about fees. They are explicitly told to consult a qualified professional before deciding how to proceed.

In each example, the shortlist is shaped by the work, not by a ranking. The checklist is the same; the weights differ.

## Step 5: Conduct short, structured conversations

A shortlist is only useful if you use it to have better conversations. Before each call, decide which two or three checklist items matter most. During the call, ask the same questions of each candidate so that your notes are comparable. After the call, write down one sentence about fit and one sentence about concerns.

You are not looking for the provider who impresses you most. You are looking for the provider whose approach matches the work you described in Step 1 and the way you want to work.

## What to avoid

- **Do not treat a directory ranking as a fit signal.** Rankings reflect visibility, not match.
- **Do not compare on price alone.** A lower initial cost can mean a narrower scope or less follow-up. Ask what is included.
- **Do not assume a large firm is always better or a solo practitioner is always more attentive.** Both can be right or wrong for a given matter.
- **Do not skip the conversation.** A shortlist is a hypothesis. The conversation is where you test it.

## When to bring in a professional

This guide is about building a shortlist, not about resolving a legal matter. If you are facing a deadline, a dispute, a confusingly similar mark already in use, or a decision with significant commercial consequences, consult a qualified professional. A shortlist is a tool for choosing whom to consult. It is not a substitute for the consultation itself.

## A short recap

Build a shortlist by defining the work, generating candidates from multiple channels, filtering for fit before quality, comparing finalists on a shared checklist, and having structured conversations. The goal is not the longest list or the most impressive name. The goal is a small set of providers who plausibly fit the work you actually have, so that your comparison is grounded in something you can evaluate.
