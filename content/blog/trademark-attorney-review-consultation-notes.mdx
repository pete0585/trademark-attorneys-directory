# Trademark Attorney: How to Review Your Consultation Notes

You finished your consultation with a trademark attorney. Maybe it was a short call, maybe it was a long meeting. Either way, you probably wrote something down — or wish you had. The notes you take immediately after a consultation are one of the most useful tools you have when deciding what to do next.

This guide walks through how to review those notes in a structured way. It is written for people who are still comparing providers and trying to decide how to move forward. It is not legal advice, and it is not a substitute for a conversation with a qualified attorney about your specific situation.

## Why reviewing notes matters more than you think

A consultation can cover a lot of ground. You might hear about the difference between common law rights and federal registration, the role of a clearance search, the distinction between a word mark and a design mark, or how the class of goods and services affects an application. In the moment, all of it can feel clear. A day later, some of it may blur.

Reviewing your notes does three things:

1. It separates what you actually heard from what you assumed.
2. It surfaces gaps you can fill with a follow-up question.
3. It gives you a fair basis for comparing one provider with another.

If you take nothing else from this article, take this: your notes are a record of what was said, not a verdict on what will happen. Treat them as a working document.

## Start with the basics: what did you actually capture?

Before you interpret anything, look at what you wrote down. A useful review starts with a quick inventory.

- Did you write the attorney's or firm's name and the date?
- Did you note who you spoke with — an attorney, a paralegal, or an intake coordinator?
- Did you capture the main topics, or only fragments?
- Did you write down any deadlines or time-sensitive items?
- Did you note anything you did not understand?

If your notes are thin, that is useful information too. It may mean you need a follow-up conversation, or that you should ask for a written summary of the discussion.

## A checklist for reviewing your consultation notes

Use this checklist as you go back through what you wrote. It is designed to be worked through in order.

### 1. Separate facts from impressions

Go through your notes and mark each item as either a fact or an impression.

- **Fact:** "The attorney said a clearance search is typically done before filing."
- **Impression:** "I felt like they were rushing."

Both are worth keeping. But they answer different questions. Facts help you compare providers. Impressions help you decide whether you want to work with someone.

### 2. Identify what was about your situation and what was general

Some comments will be specific to your mark, your goods or services, or your timeline. Others will be general explanations of how the process tends to work.

- **Specific:** "They said my proposed mark might be considered descriptive for the goods I described."
- **General:** "They explained that a search can surface earlier filings."

Specific comments are usually the ones you want to follow up on. General comments are useful context, but they may apply to many situations.

### 3. Note every question you still have

As you read, write down anything that is unclear. Do not try to resolve it from memory or by searching online. Instead, group your questions into categories:

- Questions about the process
- Questions about scope of work
- Questions about communication and timelines
- Questions about what happens if something goes wrong

This list becomes the agenda for your next conversation. If you are still comparing providers, it also becomes a fair way to compare answers.

### 4. Check for anything you may have misunderstood

It is common to mishear or misremember technical language. If a term in your notes does not make sense, flag it. Examples might include:

- "Office action"
- "Class"
- "Specimen"
- "Likelihood of confusion"

You do not need to become an expert. You just need to know which terms to ask about so you are not relying on a guess.

### 5. Look for promises that were not made

A good consultation should leave you clearer, not more certain about an outcome. If your notes suggest a guaranteed result, go back and check. Attorneys generally cannot promise a specific outcome, and a provider who does should prompt careful questions.

### 6. Write a one-paragraph summary in your own words

After you finish the checklist, write a short summary. This is for you, not for anyone else. It might look like:

> I spoke with a trademark attorney about my proposed brand name. They explained the general steps and said a search would come first. I still do not understand how the class of goods affects the application, and I want to ask about timelines and what happens if there is an issue.

That paragraph is often more useful than pages of scattered notes.

## Concrete examples (clearly hypothetical)

These examples are invented to show how the checklist works. They are not based on any real person, provider, or outcome.

### Example 1: The vague but friendly call

Suppose you wrote: "Good call. They seemed nice. Talked about searching. Said to send info."

A review would flag:

- No specific next step or deadline was recorded.
- "Searching" is not defined.
- It is unclear what information they want.

Your follow-up question might be: "What specific information do you need from me, and what happens after I send it?"

### Example 2: The detailed but confusing call

Suppose you wrote several pages, including terms like "class 25," "specimen," and "office action." You understood the general shape of the conversation but not the details.

A review would flag:

- Several technical terms are recorded but not explained.
- It is unclear which comments applied to your mark specifically.

Your follow-up question might be: "Can you walk me through which of these items applies to my situation and which are general background?"

### Example 3: The comparison call

Suppose you spoke with two providers. Your notes for the first are detailed. Your notes for the second are sparse.

A review would flag:

- You may be comparing based on uneven information.
- The second provider may deserve a follow-up call before you decide.

Your follow-up question might be: "I want to make sure I understand your approach. Can you summarize how you would handle my situation?"

## Turning notes into a decision

Once you have reviewed your notes, you are in a better position to decide what to do next. That decision usually falls into one of a few buckets:

- **Proceed with this provider.** You have enough clarity, your questions were answered, and you understand the next steps.
- **Ask follow-up questions first.** You are interested, but something is unclear or missing.
- **Compare with another provider.** You want a second perspective before committing.
- **Pause and reconsider.** Something in the consultation raised a concern, or your own priorities have shifted.

None of these is a failure. The point of reviewing notes is to make the decision deliberate rather than automatic.

## What to do if your notes reveal a problem

Sometimes a review turns up something that does not sit right. Maybe a promise was made. Maybe a question was dodged. Maybe the scope of work was never discussed.

In that case:

1. Write down the specific concern.
2. Decide whether it is a dealbreaker or a question to raise.
3. If it is a question, ask it directly in your next conversation.
4. If it is a dealbreaker, it is reasonable to look at other providers.

You are allowed to take your time. A consultation is not a commitment, and reviewing your notes is part of making an informed choice.

## A short note on record-keeping

Keep your notes somewhere you can find them. If you speak with more than one provider, label each set clearly with the date and the name of the person you spoke with. If you receive a written summary, keep it with your notes.

This is not about building a file for its own sake. It is about giving yourself a fair basis for comparison and a clear record of what you were told.

## When to consult a qualified professional

This article is about reviewing notes, not about giving legal advice. Trademark law is specific to jurisdiction, to the mark, and to the goods or services involved. If you have questions about your own situation, the right step is to speak with a qualified trademark attorney or another licensed professional in your jurisdiction.

If you are still choosing a provider, review your notes from each conversation against the same checklist. Consistency is what makes the comparison meaningful.

## Summary

Reviewing your consultation notes is a small habit with a large payoff. It helps you separate facts from impressions, identify what you still need to ask, and decide whether to proceed, follow up, or look elsewhere. Use the checklist above, write a short summary in your own words, and treat your notes as a working document rather than a final answer.

If you take one action after reading this, make it this: set aside twenty minutes to go back through your notes before your next conversation. That twenty minutes can shape the questions you ask and the decision you make.
